## Life in Saltwharf

During the weeks since his last adventure, Cyr Baggins had settled into a comfortable routine in Seaton’s Cove. He rented a modest room from a halfling family near Saltwharf, close enough that the cries of gulls and the morning clatter of the harbor usually reached his window before the rest of the household was properly awake. His hosts were kindly people, curious without being intrusive, though having one of the adventurers who had helped save Seaton’s Cove living beneath their roof remained a source of considerable interest, particularly to their youngest child. Cyr had become a familiar figure around the house as well, coming and going at odd hours, sometimes returning with the smell of the sea on his clothes or something interesting from the market in his pockets.

Saltwharf suited him. It was a working district where fishermen, sailors, dockhands and fishwives crowded narrow streets between warehouses, smokehouses and weathered homes. Boats arrived with the tide, barrels rolled across the wharves, nets hung from racks to dry, and gulls descended whenever anyone was careless enough to leave a basket uncovered. The air carried salt, tar, wet rope, fish and woodsmoke, and the noise rarely stopped until well after dark. Cyr had been around long enough that people knew him now. Dockhands called greetings as he passed, fishermen occasionally waved him over to inspect some unusual catch, and the stories of his earlier adventures had earned him a place in Saltwharf that was somewhere between local celebrity and familiar neighbor.

One of the people Cyr saw regularly was ***Lurla Netbinder***, a broad-shouldered, sea-hardened fishwife who seemed almost as permanent a fixture of Saltwharf as the docks themselves. Her faded blue oilcloth apron was usually wet or stained from the day's work, a scarf held back her grey-streaked hair, and her rough hands were seldom still as she scrubbed tables, gutted fish or gestured emphatically while arguing with fishermen over the quality of their catch. Her voice carried easily across the wharf, and more than one merchant had discovered that bargaining with Lurla was less a negotiation than a test of endurance. Yet there was something different about her these days. Her husband, Harl Netbinder, had vanished three weeks earlier while making a midnight delivery, and Lurla had not missed a tide since. Even while she worked, her gaze repeatedly wandered toward the waterline. She spoke of strange noises beneath the docks, bait barrels disappearing during the night and rats growing bold enough to sit in the open and stare back at anyone who approached.

Cyr encountered her one afternoon while passing through the fish market. Lurla was cleaning a row of silver-scaled fish with practiced strokes, but every few moments she glanced past him toward the pilings. “Rats are getting worse,” she remarked without looking up from her knife. “Big ones too. Used to scatter when somebody came near. Now some of the little bastards just sit there watching.” She scraped another fish clean and tossed it into a waiting basket. “Harl said something was moving under those docks before he disappeared. I told him it was rats.” Her knife stopped for only a moment before she resumed working. “Wish I'd listened closer.” Cyr had no answer that would make three weeks of waiting easier, and Lurla did not appear to expect one. A moment later she was shouting at a fisherman for putting yesterday's catch among the fresh fish, her grief tucked back beneath the hard shell Saltwharf knew so well.

Later that afternoon, Cyr stopped at one of the stalls on his way back to his lodgings and brought home a fresh sardine for the family's cat. He had barely stepped through the door before the animal appeared, apparently able to smell the fish through its wrapping. It wound around Cyr's boots while the family's youngest child hurried over to investigate. “That's for him, isn't it?” the child asked. Cyr crouched and lowered the sardine, only for the cat to rise onto its hind legs and reach impatiently toward his hand. The child laughed. “You're supposed to wait! He hasn't even given it to you yet.” The cat showed little interest in proper manners, and Cyr surrendered the sardine before claws became part of the negotiation.

The cat seized its prize and carried it away while the child followed, still lecturing it about its behavior. Cyr went about his evening without giving the incident another thought. Cats were everywhere in Saltwharf, slipping beneath carts, sleeping on barrels and watching fishmongers with the intense patience of creatures that understood someone would eventually drop something worth stealing. Lurla's missing husband and her stories about bold rats belonged to the more troubling side of life along the docks, but a halfling bringing home a sardine for a child's cat was ordinary.

For the moment, that was all it was.

## An Evening at the Salty Net

Two days later Cyr spent the evening at the Salty Net & Fishery Hall. The place was as crowded as usual, its long tables occupied by fishermen finishing their day's drinking, sailors between voyages, dockhands arguing over work and fishwives who seemed perfectly capable of continuing the day's bargaining long after the market had closed. Old oars, cracked floats, nets and faded ship pennants hung from the beams overhead, while the smell of ale and fish stew mixed with the hickory smoke drifting in from the smokehouses farther down the wharf. Tamily Tanderveil moved among the tables with her ledger tucked beneath one arm, keeping track of tabs and conversations with equal efficiency.

Outside, Saltwharf was beginning to show signs of the coming festival. Lanterns and shell garlands appeared along railings and doorways, painted pieces of driftwood decorated stalls, and visiting sailors and traders added unfamiliar faces to the usual harbor crowd. None of this made the district quieter. Every time the Salty Net's door opened, another burst of noise came in with the smell of the sea: gulls screaming over the roofs, men calling across the wharf and ropes creaking as the last boats of the day settled against their moorings.

The conversation around Cyr wandered from the day's catch to damaged nets, visiting ships and the usual complaints about prices. Somewhere in it came the first mention of the cats. A fisherman remarked that there seemed to be fewer of them around the sheds, which prompted someone else to mention that he had not seen a familiar tom in a couple of days. Tamily paused long enough to add that Finn and Sable had not been around either.

"Maybe they've finally found someone who feeds them better than you lot do," someone suggested.

That earned a few laughs, and the conversation moved on. Cats wandered. Saltwharf was full of sheds, warehouses and alleys, and no one thought much of two or three disappearing for a few days. Cyr had no particular reason to think differently.

## The Rat Skull

The following morning Cyr came downstairs and noticed the sardine.

It was still there.

Whether the cat had somehow lost interest in its prize or the fish had been set aside after Cyr went upstairs, it now lay untouched where the animal should easily have found it. More noticeably, there was no cat waiting beneath the table, rubbing against ankles or watching hopefully for breakfast. Cyr remembered the conversation from the Salty Net the previous evening. The absence was not enough by itself to prove anything, but the untouched fish bothered him. The animal had certainly shown no lack of interest in it before.

Later that day Cyr returned to the Salty Net. The normal afternoon business was underway when the door opened and Tellen Farshaw hurried inside. The gangly thirteen-year-old worked as a net-boy and apprentice around the Fishery Hall, carrying messages and helping with the endless work of repairing the harbor's nets. He also regarded Cyr with the particular admiration of a boy who had heard the stories of what the adventurers had done beneath Seaton's Cove. This time Tellen was not looking for an excuse to talk to him. Fish scales still clung to the boy's sleeves as he hurried between the tables with something wrapped tightly in sailcloth.

"Cyr! Cyr—wait up!"

Tellen reached him breathless and placed the bundle on the table between the tankards. He hesitated before peeling back the sailcloth. Inside was a rat's skull, yellowed and completely clean of flesh. Deep curling marks covered portions of the bone, rust-colored against the pale surface. Some appeared to have been scratched into it with a tool; others looked disturbingly as though they had been made by teeth. Whatever their origin, the marks formed repeated shapes rather than the random damage of an animal gnawing on bone.

"I found it behind the smokehouse," Tellen said, lowering his voice as though the skull might somehow hear him. "Brinehook. It was in one of the coil baskets." He glanced toward the door before continuing. "Tug wouldn't go near it. Barked at it, but he wouldn't come any closer."

Cyr turned the skull in his hands and studied the markings. They were crude, but deliberate. Tellen had more to tell him. The rats around the wharf had been behaving strangely, coming out more boldly than usual, and Tellen claimed he had seen one "standing funny." More importantly, the boy had noticed the same thing people were beginning to discuss around the Salty Net: cats were disappearing.

Now Cyr thought again of the sardine sitting untouched at his lodgings.

A few missing cats could be dismissed. The family cat failing to come home could still be coincidence. But a marked skull found behind a Saltwharf smokehouse was something else entirely. Cyr wrapped it again in the sailcloth and asked Tellen to show him exactly where he had found it.

## Behind the Brinehook

The Brinehook Smokehouse stood among the soot-blackened buildings downwind of the main wharf. Molgrim Harthorn's establishment was difficult to miss even without a sign; hickory smoke hung around the building, and the air carried the heavy smell of salt, fish oil and preserved meat. Workers and sailors passed through often enough that the ground around it held a confusion of tracks. Tellen led Cyr around the side and pointed toward a broken coil basket tucked among stacked crates, rope and salt-stained benches.

"Right there," he said. "That's where it was."

At first glance the ground offered exactly what Cyr expected from a working smokehouse beside the harbor. Boots had crossed and recrossed the area. Dogs had wandered through after scraps, cats had prowled between the crates, and the smaller tracks of ordinary rats disappeared beneath walls and piles of refuse. Cyr crouched and took his time. The useful thing about tracks was that they did not care whether anyone else thought they were important.

Gradually the confusion began to separate. He identified human footprints, dog tracks and several sets left by cats. Small rodent prints were plentiful. Among them, however, were larger impressions that did not belong to any ordinary dock rat. They appeared in several places and disappeared again where the ground became too hard to hold them. Near the broken basket Cyr noticed a faint coppery smell beneath the overpowering mixture of brine, smoke and rotting fish. Then he found something better.

Marks had been chewed into the nearby wood.

Cyr took out the wrapped skull and compared them. The shapes were not identical, but they were close enough that coincidence seemed increasingly unlikely. Something had marked both the bone and the smokehouse, and whatever had done it had been moving among the ordinary traffic of Saltwharf without attracting much attention.

Tellen watched him work for a while before asking, "You think it's rats?"

Cyr looked again at the oversized tracks and the deliberate marks cut into the wood. Ordinary rats he understood. Saltwharf had thousands of them.

"Not the sort you're thinking of."

## Beneath the Wharf

Cyr continued following what signs he could find toward the older pilings of Saltwharf. At low tide the underside of the docks became another world, separated from the busy streets above by only a few feet of timber. Warped boards crossed overhead while black water moved sluggishly around barnacled supports. Hanging nets and lengths of rope brushed against the pilings, and every surface seemed slick with seawater, fish oil or the accumulated filth of the harbor. Rats moved freely here, darting between shadows whenever Cyr approached.

The larger tracks led deeper.

Cyr moved quietly, taking advantage of the pilings and clutter rather than announcing himself. The noise of Saltwharf became strangely distant beneath the docks, reduced to muffled footsteps overhead, the creak of timbers and the slow slap of water against wood. Then a sharp hiss sounded somewhere ahead. It was not the warning squeak of a rat.

Cyr edged between two leaning supports and saw movement in the gloom. Two figures crouched among the beams, each roughly the size of a human child. Both were covered in fur and had narrow bodies, long twitching tails and clawed hands. Crude harnesses of rope and bone crossed their chests. One held a net glistening with fish oil while the other carried a small whistle.

Several cats were nearby.

The creature with the whistle raised it to its mouth and blew a single sharp note. The cats froze just long enough for its companion to throw the net. The weighted mesh dropped across them amid an explosion of hissing, claws and tangled fur. The two creatures chittered excitedly and moved toward their struggling catch.

Cyr had found what was taking Saltwharf's cats.

## The Ratling Hunters

Cyr did not give them time to carry the animals away. He came out of concealment and attacked, turning the cramped space beneath the wharf into a sudden, vicious fight. The ratlings were small, but they were neither helpless nor surprised for long. One abandoned the trapped cats and tried to use its net against Cyr while the other maneuvered among the pilings, taking advantage of the narrow beams and shallow tidal water.

The fight ended with one of the ratlings dead. The surviving creature stared at Cyr for an instant, its expression carrying more hatred than panic, before abandoning its companion and scrambling away. It squeezed between the pilings and disappeared into a narrow drainage passage leading beneath the town. Cyr could hear its claws for several moments before even that sound faded.

He turned back to the net. Once freed, the trapped cats needed no encouragement to escape. They scattered beneath the wharf and vanished through whatever routes generations of Saltwharf cats had learned to use.

Cyr now had an answer to one question. The cats had not simply wandered away. Someone was taking them, and the creatures doing it possessed tools, worked together and had somewhere beneath Seaton's Cove to retreat.

## The Cat Who Walked With Shadows

Cyr was still beneath the wharf when he realized that he and the ratlings had not been alone.

A large feline shape sat on one of the higher crossbeams, silhouetted against the thin light filtering between the dock planks above. It was much too large to be one of Saltwharf's ordinary cats. Its fur seemed almost to drink the surrounding light, leaving only a faint suggestion of silver smoke around its outline. Behind it, two long tails moved slowly and independently.

The fleeing ratling saw it as well. Halfway into the drainage passage, the creature glanced upward and released a shrill hiss that sounded unmistakably frightened before vanishing into the darkness.

The feline remained.

It descended one beam and then another without making a sound. Cyr could see its eyes now, golden and reflective, but there was something in them beyond the alertness of an animal. The creature watched him with an intelligence he could not easily dismiss. It did not approach like a pet seeking food or retreat like a wild animal confronted by a stranger. It simply studied him.

For several moments Cyr and the strange cat regarded one another beneath the wharf. One of its tails curled slowly behind it. Then a shadow passed across the timbers, and when Cyr looked again, the creature was gone.

Something lay where it had been.

Cyr picked up a worn leather collar with a small brass bell attached. The leather was broken and the bell dented. It belonged to one of the cats that had been seen around Saltwharf before the disappearances began.

## Proof in the Lanternlight

Cyr did not leave the dead ratling beneath the docks. He carried the body to the Saltwharf guard post, where its appearance immediately stopped whatever routine business had occupied the watch. Under the lanterns there was no mistaking the creature for an ordinary rat. It had narrow hands, fur, long incisors, a tail and the crude bone trinkets that had hung from its harness.

One of the guards stared at it. "That a goblin?"

Another leaned closer before shaking his head. "No. That ain't any goblin I've ever seen."

Word brought Constable Garran Pike, Saltwharf's thick-set former dockhand turned watchman. Pike knew the ward and its people well and was accustomed to handling everything from drunken sailors and smugglers to fights over cargo, but the corpse Cyr had deposited before him was plainly outside the usual run of harbor trouble. He crouched beside it, examined the harness and bone fetishes, and then looked up at Cyr.

"Where?"

"Beneath the wharf," Cyr told him. "There were two. They were trapping cats."

Pike's expression tightened. "Two?"

"One got away."

That was enough to change the constable's concern. One strange creature could have found its way into town. Two creatures working together, carrying equipment and taking animals for some unknown purpose suggested something established beneath Saltwharf.

Lurla Netbinder was among those drawn to the commotion. The normally formidable fishwife became noticeably quieter when she saw the body. Whatever doubts she might have had about the strange things being reported around the ward, there was now something solid lying in the lanternlight.

Oswald Percival Brinehart arrived amid the gathering as well, perfumed and dressed far too richly for the working wharf. He recoiled when he saw the ratling and then, almost as quickly, became aware of everyone else who was watching.

"Gods preserve us. What vile dock-fiend is this?" Oswald asked. He circled the corpse without coming close enough to touch it. After a moment his expression changed. "I suspected something foul was about. Saw movement near my warehouses only last eve."

Lurla looked at him.

Oswald cleared his throat. "Confronted one myself, as a matter of fact. Drove the thing off."

"You trip over your own boots in daylight, Oswald," Lurla said.

One of the guards found something suddenly fascinating about the opposite wall.

## Oswald Brinehart, Ratling Slayer

By the following day, Oswald's encounter with the ratlings had improved considerably.

Cyr heard several versions as he moved through Saltwharf. In one, Oswald had encountered a rat-creature near a warehouse and driven it away. In another there had been several creatures. Somewhere along the way they acquired knives, and Oswald acquired a crate hook. By the time the story reached some of the dockworkers, the wealthy merchant had apparently defended a significant portion of Saltwharf almost single-handedly.

Most people who actually knew Oswald reacted accordingly. Tamily rolled her eyes when his name came up. Dockhands made wagers over how many ratlings he would have slain by supper. Lurla's opinion required considerably less imagination.

The problem was that Oswald Percival Brinehart was not merely a harmless tavern braggart. He owned Brinehart Imports & Fine Provisions in Lanterncross and dealt in expensive textiles, spices, perfumes, wines and foreign curios. He knew ship captains, harbor officials, caravan masters and members of the town council. His vanity might make his claims ridiculous, but his money and connections ensured that people listened to him.

Cyr, meanwhile, showed little interest in competing for ownership of the story.

## The Morning After

When Cyr returned to the Salty Net, Tamily was already at work behind the long table with her ledger open. She looked up as he approached and gave him the expression of someone who had spent the morning hearing increasingly stupid things.

"I heard Oswald slew a sewer demon," she said. "Last version had three of them."

Cyr told her what had actually happened. He described following the signs beneath the wharf, finding the two ratlings trapping cats, killing one and watching the other escape into the drainage passage. He told her about the creature's equipment and the fact that the pair had been working deliberately rather than scavenging like animals.

Then he told her about the cat.

Tamily's amusement disappeared as Cyr described the oversized feline sitting among the beams, its two tails and strange golden eyes, and the terror it had inspired in the escaping ratling. The creature had watched Cyr after the fight and then vanished without explanation.

Finally Cyr took out the broken collar and bell.

Tamily turned it over in her hand. The worn leather and dented brass were ordinary enough, which somehow made them more troubling than the marked skull or the strange creature beneath the docks. This had belonged to an animal people in Saltwharf knew. It had not wandered off in search of better scraps.

Someone had taken it.

## The Broken Bell

Cyr took the collar back and considered everything that had happened over the past several days. The strange behavior of the rats, the disappearing cats and the untouched sardine had seemed like unrelated details at first. The marked skull changed that. The tracks behind the Brinehook had given those suspicions direction, and what he found beneath the wharf had confirmed that something organized was operating beneath Saltwharf.

The ratlings were intelligent enough to use nets and whistles. They had deliberately captured cats rather than killed them where they found them. At least one passage beneath the wharf led somewhere deeper beneath Seaton's Cove, and one of the creatures now knew that Cyr had discovered them.

Then there was the other cat.

It had not behaved like anything Cyr knew from the wharf. It had watched the fight rather than joining it or fleeing, and the ratling's reaction suggested that their paths had crossed before. Whatever the two-tailed creature was, it had allowed Cyr to see it and had left the broken collar behind.

Whether that was warning, invitation or simple coincidence, Cyr could not yet say.

## Cyr Contemplates

Cyr sat with the little brass bell in his hand and let the noise of the Salty Net carry on around him. Five days earlier, Saltwharf had been ordinary: Lurla shouting across the fish market, Tamily working her tables, boats coming into the harbor, and a child laughing while the family cat tried to steal a sardine from Cyr's fingers. Now that cat was gone, and beneath the same familiar streets Cyr had found creatures stealing animals away into the darkness.

He turned the dented bell between his fingers. Somewhere beneath Seaton's Cove was a ratling that had escaped him. Somewhere in the town, Oswald Brinehart was probably killing another three of them with every telling. And somewhere beyond both of those concerns walked a strange two-tailed cat that had watched Cyr fight, frightened the ratlings, and left him with one small piece of the mystery.

For the moment, Cyr did not know which trail he should follow first.
