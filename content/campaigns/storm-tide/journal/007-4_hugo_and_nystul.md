## Hugo and Nystul: The Tinkerer

As the others began making their way out of the cavern and back through the cabin above, **Hugo** heard the familiar sound again.

*Click… whirr… grind.*

He stopped near **Nystul** and glanced toward the apparently empty space beside him. “That sound Xeril makes,” Hugo said. “It isn’t supposed to make that sound, is it?”

Nystul regarded him with mild skepticism. “Xeril makes a great many sounds.”

“Not that one. Something is catching.”

Hugo offered to fetch his tools and take a look. Nystul hesitated, clearly unconvinced that a young wizard with a tinkerer’s kit was the answer to a problem in one of his own constructs, but curiosity finally overcame caution.

“Very well,” he said. “Look first.”

At the cabin table, Nystul allowed Xeril to become visible and brought the little brass-and-iron construct down to working height. Hugo listened carefully, opened one of the small access plates, and expected to spend some time tracing gears and linkages.

Instead, he found the problem almost immediately.

A tiny lever had been bent just far enough out of alignment to scrape against the edge of its guide with every turn of the mechanism.

Hugo placed the tip of a small tool against it and applied the slightest pressure.

*Tick.*

The lever slipped back into place.

Xeril’s gears turned.

*Click… whirr…*

Silence.

The construct rose smoothly above the table and faded from sight. This time there was no grinding sound to betray its position.

For perhaps the first time since arriving at Wreckshore, Nystul seemed genuinely at a loss for words. He extended one hand, and the invisible Xeril drifted toward him in perfect silence.

“I thought only I could mend it,” Nystul said at last. “Perhaps I was wrong.”

He looked from Hugo’s tools back to Hugo.

“Perhaps you are not so ordinary after all.”

From somewhere above Nystul’s hand came one soft, contented *whirr*.

Hugo smiled.

“That one sounds normal.”

## Weeks Later

Weeks later, a courier arrived at the cabin carrying a small wooden box wrapped carefully for travel. There was no visible lock, latch, or keyhole. Where a lock should have been, a single symbol had been engraved into the wood: an eye-like sigil surrounded by four sharp points.

The courier also carried a rolled parchment secured with a dark red wax seal impressed with the same mark.

“From my master,” he said when asked. “Nystul. I was instructed to place both in the hands of **Hugo Baudelaire, the Tinkerer**.”

That last word caught Hugo’s attention.

He broke the wax seal and unrolled the message.

> **Speak the name of my clockwork assistant that you repaired to open the box.**

Hugo looked down at the engraved symbol, then at the box.

“Xeril.”

There was a faint metallic *click* from somewhere inside.

The engraved sigil briefly glimmered, and a seam appeared along the lid where none had been visible before. The box opened without a key.

Inside lay a carefully arranged collection of scrolls, loose sheets, diagrams, and handwritten notes. These were not copied spells from some academy library or a collection intended for general study. They were Nystul’s own work—personal spells he had developed and refined over years of research.

Among them were **Nystul’s Flash**, **Nystul’s Blazing Beam**, **Nystul’s Crystal Dagger**, and **Nystul’s Radiant Baton**.

Hugo stood quietly for a time, turning over the first few pages.

There was no long letter of praise. No elaborate explanation. Nystul had simply sent him something far more meaningful: access to his work.

The message was clear enough.

The repair of Xeril had been remembered.
